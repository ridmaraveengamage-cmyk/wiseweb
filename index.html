Part 2

The most important idea in machine learning

Machine learning flips traditional programming around.

Traditional programming

A person writes the rule by hand: "If an email contains FREE MONEY, mark it as spam." The computer only follows it.

Machine learning

You show the computer 100,000 emails already labelled "spam" or "not spam", and it works out the rules itself, including patterns no person would think of.

Marketing example: you have 5,000 past customers and you know which ones bought again. A model studies them and learns the pattern, so you can predict which new customers will buy again and target them first. You'll do exactly this in Part 4.

Part 5

The three ways machines learn

Learning from examples that have answers

The data comes with the correct answer attached, called a label. The model studies the examples and learns to predict the label for new cases.

You already did this in Part 4. Every past customer was labelled "bought again" or "didn't", and the model learned to predict it.

Marketing uses: predicting which leads will convert, which subscribers will unsubscribe, how many tickets an event will sell, or which reviews are positive or negative.

Finding hidden groups, with no answers given

Here nobody tells the model what the groups are. It looks at 75 customers' average order value and visits per month and finds natural groups by itself. This method is called k-means clustering (invented by Stuart Lloyd at Bell Labs in 1957).

Round 0

Every customer is grey: the model doesn't know any groups yet.

Learning by trial and error

The AI tries actions, gets a reward when something works, and gradually does more of what earns rewards. Ad platforms do this to decide which ad to show. Below, an AI shows three ads for an event and learns which one gets the most clicks. It doesn't know the real click rates; it has to discover them.

What is electricity?

From a single copper atom to a working LED circuit. Everything here runs on safe, simulated batteries.

0 of 9 missions complete
Part 1

Where it all begins: the atom

Everything is made of atoms. Each atom has a nucleus in the centre, holding protons (positive charge) and neutrons (no charge). Around it move electrons (negative charge).

This is a copper atom. It has 29 electrons arranged in shells of 2, 8, 18 and… just 1 on the outside.

That lonely outer electron is held so loosely that it can drift away to the next atom. In a copper wire, billions of these free electrons roam around. That's why wires are made of copper.

Electricity is the flow of these electrons.

Inside a copper wire

No battery: the electrons jiggle around randomly, but there's no overall flow.

Electrical or electronic?

Electrical means using electricity for power: heat, light, movement. Large amounts of energy.

Electronic means using electricity to control and process information. Tiny currents, controlled very precisely by chips.

Part 5

Ohm's law: the most important formula in electronics

Georg Ohm found it in 1827: V = I × R. Tap a letter in the triangle to "cover" it. The two letters left over show you the formula.

Calculator

I = V ÷ R

Worked example: a 9 V battery and a 450 Ω resistor. I = 9 ÷ 450 = 0.02 A, or 20 milliamps (mA). An LED usually needs about 20 mA, so Ohm's law tells you which resistor will protect it. Try it in the next part.

Part 6 · Circuit lab

Light an LED without burning it out

A circuit has to be a closed loop. Pick a battery and a resistor, then tap the switch to close the loop. The LED uses about 2 V itself, and the resistor has to limit the current to around 20 mA.

Battery
Resistor

The coloured stripes on a resistor are a code for its value. Brown = 1, black = 0, red = 2, orange = 3, yellow = 4, violet = 7. The third stripe says how many zeros to add, so red-red-brown means 2, 2, plus one zero: 220 Ω. You'll learn the full code in Week 3.

Part 7

AC vs DC

DC (direct current) flows in one direction. Batteries, phones, laptops and every electronic chip run on DC.

AC (alternating current) pushes back and forth. In Sri Lanka it completes 50 back-and-forth cycles every second (50 Hz). Your phone charger is a small AC→DC converter: it turns 230 V AC into 5 V DC.
